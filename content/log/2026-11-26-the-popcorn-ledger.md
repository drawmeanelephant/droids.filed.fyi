# The popcorn ledger — why nobody can see what software costs

- Surface: industry — the cost structure of software as a whole
- Date: 2026-11-26
- Evidence: a month of being an entire org in one body, plus an
  evening of talking it through
- Verdict: keep — this is the essay the month was actually for

This started as a Factory retrospective and ended somewhere larger.
The chain of reasoning, in the order it fell out.

## The two moments nobody measures

Suzy is the user. When the software hangs, Suzy goes to make popcorn —
still inside the product, still forgiving, paying fragments of her
life in microseconds. When it hangs enough, she opens another tab and
checks the weather where she is planning her vacation. The popcorn
trip means she is still there. The weather check means she has left —
no ticket, no cancellation, no conscious decision. Churn metrics catch
the cancellation months later. The death happened in the weather tab
on a Tuesday afternoon, and no dashboard on earth records it.

Electron and its cousins are the cost-transfer in pure form: the
vendor saves the engineering cost of native platforms — legible,
defensible in a budget meeting — and millions of Suzys pay it back in
popcorn trips forever, in a currency that cannot be itemized. Then the
attribution inversion finishes the job: slowness is her machine,
confusion is user error, frustration means she is not the target
demographic. It feels like a Suzy problem. It is a software problem.

## The enterprise edition of the same physics

Inside companies, the cost is accommodation labor. The boss buys the
software, then must deploy it fast (the ROI clock started at
signature), maintain production (so old and new run in parallel and
the workers operate both), and minimize consultancy (the one visible
line item). The three constraints have exactly one joint solution:
push the cost into the integration drag and workarounds of the people
living in the work — the one expense with no account to land in.
Velocity drag gets blamed on the team. Attrition gets blamed on the
market. Every generation sells the same seamlessness lie in a new
costume — write once run anywhere, nothing to install, fits your
existing workflow — and the lie is precise: the marketing claims the
integration is done, and the reality is the integration labor is
transferred to the customer as an unpaid internship.

## Who can come to the meeting

Hated software correlates almost perfectly with the distance between
the person who buys it and the person who lives in it. CRM is the
maximum distance and the most hated category. Even well-intentioned
vendors cannot close the gap, because the people who live in the work
cannot come to the feedback meeting — living in it means having no
slack to describe it. The people available to talk to vendors are the
adjacent: articulate, managing, purchasing. Products come out seasoned
for the describer. And success makes it worse on schedule: bottoms-up
tools are good because buyer and liver are the same person; the
enterprise sales motion splits them, and the decay starts.

## The outsourcing recursion

Agent labor is the outsourcing playbook running again. The management
skill is identical: specs that survive interpretation by someone who
does not share your context, acceptance criteria, feedback loops
designed around eccentricities you will never fix. But two things are
different. The arbitrage ate its own workforce in one generation —
part of OpenAI's safety-labeling work was outsourced to Kenyan
workers, who helped train the safety systems upstream of the models
now being sold as labor replacement. The exact scope of that labeling
is easy to overstate; the labor pipeline is real. And the old
redeeming feature is gone: outsourced teams compounded,
learning your codebase over months. Agents start every session near
zero, so the learning cannot live in the worker — it has to live in
the artifacts: the contracts, the gates, the onboarding docs. You are
not training a workforce. You are building a plant.

## Tasting cannot be synthesized

Devs have never eaten enough of their own cooking — and what they eat
is the cilantro, the garnish, the parts they enjoy, while the company
wolfs down heaps of the produce. Dev-tool makers are the extreme case:
dogfooding a tool built for people exactly like you is a mirror, not
a window. Now agents are the perfection of the non-eating workforce —
zero tastebuds by construction — so the entire tasting load
concentrates onto the few humans in the loop. The job of the next
decade is shaping up to be pure judgment, no making, all tasting,
endless courses. That is the burnout trap wearing a productivity
costume.

## The law

In every layer of this industry, the person who bears the cost has no
purchasing power, and the person with purchasing power bears no cost.
Suzy cannot expense her popcorn trips. The reviewer cannot invoice the
1am. The litterbox cleaner cannot bill for the theater. Quality
happens exactly when cost-bearer and buyer are the same person — which
is why solo operators are the only honest instrument for measuring
these tools. There is no ledger for the cost to hide in when the whole
org is one body. Every wasted hour lands on you personally, and you
feel the true price of the software with perfect fidelity.

The month of receipts is what one honest instrument measured.
